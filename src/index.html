Is the Economy Producing?

A recession is not announced by a rule. It is decided by a committee reading a handful of monthly series, and industrial production is one of them. That makes output the thing a recession partly is, which means it cannot warn you: on this page's own numbers its crest arrives a median … months from the recession peak and its alarm arrives … months after. Beneath that sits the rule the public uses instead, two consecutive negative quarters, scored against the same committee. It false-alarms, it misses, and it has never once led. Nothing here is a forecast.

The rule you have heard: two negative quarters

Everyone repeats it and the NBER has never used it. The committee dates cycles on depth, diffusion and duration across several monthly series, and it says so publicly. So here is the folk rule computed on real GDP since 1947 and scored against the committee's own dates, on exactly the terms the episode tables above and on the sibling sites use. The heavy rule is zero.

And the recessions it never noticed

The misses matter more than the false alarm, because this rule is used as a definition rather than a forecast. These are recessions the committee dated and the folk rule never fired for at all. The quarters around each peak are printed so you can see exactly how it failed to trigger.

2001 is the case that should end the argument. The negatives were there, in the first and third quarters, but a positive second quarter sat between them, so two never landed in a row and the rule stayed silent through a recession nobody disputes.

The 2022 argument, and what the data says now

In July 2022 the second consecutive negative quarter printed and the country spent a summer arguing about whether a recession had begun. Both sides were reasoning from numbers that no longer exist. Capacity, and what output is made of

Capacity utilization asks a different question from production: not how much is being made, but how much of what could be made is. Manufacturing utilization runs from 1948, nineteen years deeper than the total-industry series, which is why it is the one drawn here. The components beneath it show that treating output as a single thing hides most of the story.

What the committee actually watches

The NBER's dating committee names four monthly indicators. Three of them are here, year over year, alongside industrial production: real manufacturing and trade sales, real personal income excluding transfers, and payroll employment. Drawn together rather than scored, because the point is what the committee is looking at, not another table.

The unemployment side of the same question has its own page at jobs, with the deep US and Canadian history back to the 1920s.

Canada: deep quarterly, shallow monthly

Canada publishes a monthly GDP estimate, which the United States does not, but only from 1997. The quarterly national accounts reach 1961. Both are drawn, on their own axes and never spliced, because a 1961 quarterly series and a 1997 monthly one are different measurements and joining them would invent thirty-six years of monthly detail that was never collected. Shading is the C.D. Howe chronology.

Expect this card to be busy. Industrial production is benchmarked annually and can move years of history at once. Real GDP is revised three times for every quarter and again each annual update. The card above showing a negative quarter turning positive is not an anomaly, it is this process working normally, and it is the reason every value on this site carries its provenance and every refresh is diffed against the one before it.

Sources and construction

United States

  • Production: Federal Reserve G.17 industrial production INDPRO, monthly since January 1919, with components for manufacturing (1972), mining (1919), utilities (1939), final products (1939) and business equipment (1947). The downturn table computes from the total.
  • Capacity: CUMFNS manufacturing utilization from January 1948, preferred to TCU total industry (1967) for nineteen extra years on the same question. Both ship in the payload.
  • Output: real GDP GDPC1, quarterly since 1947-Q1. The two-quarter table scores the quarter-over-quarter change, computed here and deliberately not annualized so the figures in the table are the actual quarterly changes; the sign, which is all the rule depends on, is identical either way.
  • The committee's other indicators: CMRMTSPL real manufacturing and trade sales (1967), W875RX1 real personal income less transfers (1959), PAYEMS payrolls (1939).
  • Vintages: the as-published 2022 readings come from ALFRED at vintage 1922-07-29, fetched once and stored, because history as published on a past date does not change. ALFRED is keyed-only.
  • A stated gap: there is no free purchasing managers' index. FRED's NAPM returns 404; the ISM withdrew redistribution rights. No proxy is substituted for it.
  • Recession bands: NBER dates via USREC, from the shared dataset. The committee's own description of how it dates cycles is published here, including that it does not use the two-quarter rule.

Canada

  • Quarterly: GDP at market prices, chained 2017 dollars, SAAR, StatCan table 36-10-0104-01, vector v62305752, from 1961-Q1 and live.
  • Monthly: GDP by industry, table 36-10-0434-01, from January 1997: industrial production (v65201219), all industries (v65201210) and goods-producing industries (v65201211). Manufacturing shipments from table 16-10-0047-01, vector v800450, from 1992 and nominal.
  • Every vector's English title is verified against the live table on each fetch, so a renumbered vector fails loudly instead of returning someone else's numbers.
  • Recession bands: C.D. Howe Institute Business Cycle Council chronology, from the shared dataset.

Plumbing

  • FRED is fetched keyless-first (public CSV), keyed API as fallback; StatCan WDS has no keys. A failed fetch carries the stored series forward and says so. Only the vintage card requires a key, and it degrades to a stated gap without one.
  • Refreshes daily via cron. Industrial production lands mid-month, GDP three times per quarter, and the Canadian monthly series on StatCan's own calendar. Most runs verify rather than change; the stamp at the top says when the data last moved.
  • Both rules were tuned once against the canonical record at build time and then frozen. They ship inside the payload and are printed above their tables, because a different rule gives a different table and you should be able to see which one produced these.
  • Series follow the shared econ-core contract used across consumer, credit, lending, housing, yield, freight, jobs, debt and diesel.
  • Raw data: /api/data, or the files under /data/.
  • Assembled with Claude (Anthropic). Published indices and published aggregates, with computed history and both rules printed; no forecasts.
